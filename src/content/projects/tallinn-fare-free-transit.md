## Research question

In 2013, Tallinn eliminated public transport fares for all registered residents and branded itself the "Capital of Free Public Transport," framing the move as a sustainability intervention. Does the evidence support that framing?

## Argument

Fare-free public transport (FFPT) is now used in nearly 300 localities worldwide, and Tallinn was among the first major cities to adopt it. This paper argues that Tallinn's programme used the language of sustainable development to legitimize what was fundamentally a fiscal and political project, one that prioritized economic metrics over ecological outcomes, relied on techno-managerial solutions in place of environmental accountability, and ultimately delivered real social benefits while leaving car dependency fully intact.

## What the evidence shows

- **Political origins**: the policy was announced in a tabloid, bypassed Tallinn's own transport strategy, and was ratified by a non-binding referendum with 15% turnout, excluding the planning expertise that would have pushed for complementary measures like congestion pricing.
- **Who benefits**: FFPT is available only to registered residents, which drove roughly 25,000 people to register in Tallinn between 2012 and 2016 without relocating, pulling tax revenue from surrounding municipalities that still had to serve them.
- **Environmental accountability**: despite installing the technical infrastructure (smart cards, passenger counters, vehicle tracking) to evaluate the programme's environmental impact, no formal evaluation was ever conducted, and car-kilometres increased 31% after implementation.
- **Beyond Tallinn**: the same pattern (ridership gains without reduced car use) recurs in Luxembourg and Hasselt, Belgium, suggesting FFPT's appeal to city governments is electoral, not evidentiary. Free transit is popular and, once framed as sustainable, difficult to oppose.
